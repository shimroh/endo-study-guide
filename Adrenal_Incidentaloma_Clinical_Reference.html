Adrenal disease

Adrenal Incidentaloma

A decision-first reference for evaluating an incidentally discovered adrenal mass, distinguishing benign from malignant disease, identifying hormone excess, selecting patients for surgery, and avoiding unnecessary surveillance.
1-mg DSTNoncontrast HUMetanephrinesARRMACSAdrenal MDT
Clinical content reviewed against 2023 ESE/ENSAT and 2022 AAES guidance • Source chapter updated May 2024

Overview

Every adrenal incidentaloma generates two simultaneous questions: Could this be malignant? and Is it secreting hormone? Size alone answers neither.

Definition

An adrenal mass ≥1 cm discovered on imaging performed for a reason unrelated to suspected adrenal disease. A lesion found during cancer staging or evaluation of an endocrine syndrome is not a classic incidentaloma and carries a different pretest probability.

Common

Prevalence rises with age and cardiometabolic disease. Most lesions are benign cortical adenomas; bilateral lesions occur in roughly 10–15%.

Potentially consequential

Adrenocortical carcinoma (ACC), pheochromocytoma, primary aldosteronism, overt cortisol excess, and clinically relevant mild autonomous cortisol secretion (MACS).

Never biopsy first. Adrenal biopsy cannot reliably distinguish adenoma from ACC and can precipitate a lethal catecholamine crisis if pheochromocytoma has not been excluded. Its narrow role is usually confirmation of metastasis, lymphoma, infection, or another noncortical process when histology will change management.

Rapid workflow: the first 10 minutes

1. Verify what the scan actually shows. Side, maximal diameter, homogeneity, native (noncontrast) HU, margins, calcification, hemorrhage/necrosis, invasion, prior imaging, and whether the study was truly noncontrast or an adrenal protocol.
2. Screen clinically. Cortisol phenotype and comorbidities; episodic catecholamine symptoms; hypertension/hypokalemia; virilization/feminization; malignancy history; hereditary risk; medications that corrupt biochemical testing.
3. Order the core hormonal evaluation selectively. 1-mg overnight dexamethasone suppression test (DST) in almost all appropriate patients; plasma free or urinary fractionated metanephrines unless imaging is unequivocally lipid-rich (≤10 HU on unenhanced CT) under 2023 ESE guidance; aldosterone/renin ratio (ARR) if hypertension or unexplained hypokalemia.
4. Classify the imaging phenotype. Homogeneous and ≤10 HU → benign, regardless of size, in patients without known extra-adrenal cancer. Everything else requires risk stratification, not a reflex label of “adenoma.”
5. Decide disposition. No follow-up for clearly benign, nonfunctioning lesions; MDT/additional imaging for indeterminate lesions; endocrine surgery for hormone-producing tumors or concerning malignancy phenotype.
High-yield rule: A homogeneous mass with unenhanced CT attenuation ≤10 HU needs no additional adrenal imaging under 2023 ESE/ENSAT guidance—even if it is >4 cm. Confirm that the number is truly from the noncontrast phase and the lesion is genuinely homogeneous.

History and examination

Targeted history

  • Weight trajectory, bruising, proximal weakness, fractures, infections, menstrual change, hirsutism/acne, mood/cognitive change
  • Hypertension onset/severity; diabetes, dyslipidemia, obesity, cardiovascular disease, osteoporosis
  • Paroxysmal or sustained headache, palpitations, diaphoresis, pallor, tremor, panic-like spells, orthostasis
  • Hypokalemia, muscle cramps/weakness, polyuria/polydipsia, resistant or early-onset hypertension
  • Rapid virilization or feminization; new gynecomastia, testicular atrophy, deepening voice
  • Known malignancy, constitutional symptoms, infection or immunosuppression
  • Prior imaging: the most useful “test” may be a scan from years earlier
  • Family/personal history: pheochromocytoma/paraganglioma, MEN2, VHL, NF1, SDHx, Li-Fraumeni, Lynch, FAP, BWS, Carney complex

Examination

  • Accurate BP, including orthostatics when relevant; pulse and rhythm
  • Cushing phenotype: wide violaceous striae, thin skin/easy bruising, proximal myopathy, facial plethora, dorsocervical/supraclavicular fat
  • Androgen or estrogen excess phenotype
  • Features of syndromic disease: mucosal neuromas, café-au-lait lesions, neurofibromas
  • Body composition and fracture/height-loss clues
MACS usually lacks classic Cushing stigmata. The signal is often disproportionate or worsening hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, obesity, cardiovascular disease, or osteoporosis—not a “Cushingoid” appearance.

Second-line modalities

Adrenal-protocol washout CT

Adenomas often enhance and wash out quickly. Traditional thresholds are absolute percentage washout (APW) >60% or relative percentage washout (RPW) >40% at 10–15 minutes. These cutoffs are imperfect: pheochromocytoma and malignancy may overlap, and contemporary validation has challenged their reliability. Use as one component, not a verdict.

MRI chemical-shift imaging

Signal loss on out-of-phase relative to in-phase images supports intracellular lipid and an adenoma. Useful when avoiding radiation or iodinated contrast. Lipid-poor adenoma may not suppress; rare clear-cell RCC metastases and ACC may show signal loss.

18F-FDG PET/CT

Uptake less than liver favors benignity; uptake greater than liver raises concern. False positives include functioning adenomas and pheochromocytoma; false negatives include small lesions and some indolent malignancies. Particularly helpful for indeterminate lesions, cancer staging, or suspected ACC.

Prior imaging

Long-term stability strongly supports benign behavior but does not rescue an initially suspicious phenotype. Conversely, measurable growth can occur in benign adenomas. Interpret growth with morphology and measurement error.

Do not call a portal-venous-phase HU “unenhanced HU.” A routine contrast CT often cannot classify an adrenal lesion. Obtain or reconstruct the right study rather than building an algorithm on the wrong number.

Confirm the signal is real

  • Measure morning ACTH; low/suppressed ACTH supports adrenal autonomy. DHEA-S may be low from chronic ACTH suppression but is age- and assay-dependent.
  • Repeat DST if unexpected or management-changing. A simultaneous dexamethasone concentration can uncover inadequate ingestion, malabsorption, or rapid metabolism.
  • Review CYP3A4 inducers (e.g., phenytoin, carbamazepine, phenobarbital, rifampin) that lower dexamethasone exposure and cause false-positive DST.
  • Estrogen-containing therapy raises cortisol-binding globulin and total cortisol; oral estrogen is a common source of false-positive DST. Pregnancy, acute illness, alcohol use disorder, severe depression, and disrupted sleep can also confound.
  • Do not reflexively substitute 24-hour urinary free cortisol or late-night salivary cortisol for DST in MACS; they are frequently normal and are designed for overt Cushing syndrome physiology.

What MACS changes

Systematically evaluate hypertension, type 2 diabetes/prediabetes, dyslipidemia, obesity, cardiovascular disease, osteoporosis/vertebral fracture, and thrombotic/infectious risk when clinically relevant. Optimize standard therapy regardless of whether surgery is chosen.

Surgery for MACS is not automatic. Discuss adrenalectomy for a unilateral lesion when cortisol-related comorbidities are clinically important—especially if new, progressive, difficult to control, or disproportionate to age—and ACTH-independent cortisol secretion is persistent. Age, frailty, operative risk, degree of cortisol nonsuppression, end-organ damage, and patient preference matter. Evidence suggests metabolic benefit in selected patients, but outcome prediction remains imperfect.

Pheochromocytoma

Who to screen

Screen any adrenal lesion not conclusively typical of a benign adenoma, and any patient with catecholamine symptoms or hereditary risk. Under ESE 2023, metanephrines may be omitted for a homogeneous lesion ≤10 HU on true noncontrast CT because pheochromocytoma is exceptionally unlikely. If native HU is unavailable, do not assume.

Test choice

  • Plasma free metanephrines: high sensitivity; best collected supine after ≥20–30 minutes of quiet rest with an indwelling catheter when feasible.
  • 24-hour urinary fractionated metanephrines: reasonable alternative when optimal plasma collection is impractical; verify collection adequacy.

Interpretation

  • Marked elevation (commonly ≥3× upper reference limit) is rarely false positive and warrants localization/management.
  • Mild elevation is common: repeat under controlled conditions and review medications, stress, illness, OSA, withdrawal, nicotine/caffeine, and assay method.
  • Tricyclics/SNRIs and sympathomimetics can elevate results; acetaminophen and other analytical interferences depend on assay.
  • Consider clonidine suppression for persistent borderline normetanephrine elevation in an appropriate specialist setting; it is not useful for isolated metanephrine elevation.
Before biopsy or surgery: if pheochromocytoma remains possible, biochemically exclude it or manage it as pheochromocytoma. A “silent” tumor is not hemodynamically silent under anesthesia or needle manipulation.

Primary aldosteronism

Screen with aldosterone and renin in patients with hypertension or unexplained hypokalemia. An adrenal nodule does not prove that the nodule is producing aldosterone; incidental nonfunctioning adenomas are common.

Before the ARR

  • Correct hypokalemia, which can suppress aldosterone and cause a false-negative screen.
  • Avoid sodium restriction immediately before testing; follow local protocol for posture and collection time.
  • Interpret the absolute aldosterone, the degree of renin suppression, and the ratio—not the ratio alone.
  • Medication effects: mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists and ENaC inhibitors raise renin and may obscure disease; diuretics, ACE inhibitors, and ARBs can cause false negatives; β-blockers and central α2-agonists suppress renin and can cause false-positive ratios. A complete washout is not always safe or necessary; interpret intelligently and repeat strategically.

After a positive screen

Confirmatory suppression testing may be appropriate unless the phenotype is unequivocal under the applicable guideline/local protocol. If pursuing surgery, establish laterality with adrenal venous sampling (AVS) in most adults. CT anatomy alone misclassifies laterality because nonfunctioning nodules and bilateral aldosterone production are common. A carefully selected young patient (often <35 years) with spontaneous hypokalemia, marked aldosterone excess, suppressed renin, and a solitary unilateral adenoma with a normal contralateral gland may be considered for surgery without AVS, but this exception is narrow.

“Aldosterone-producing adenoma on CT” is not a radiologic diagnosis. It is a biochemical diagnosis plus lateralization.

Androgens, estrogens, precursors, and other tests

Do not order a random “complete adrenal panel” for every lipid-rich adenoma. Targeted testing is appropriate when the phenotype or imaging raises concern.

Management by category

Clearly benign + nonfunctioning

No adrenal surgery and no routine repeat imaging. Do not create a five-year surveillance program for a problem already solved.

Indeterminate imaging

Use dedicated adrenal radiology review and MDT. Choose one purposeful next step: immediate additional imaging, 6–12-month interval imaging, or surgery. Avoid serial tests without a predefined decision threshold.

Hormone-producing lesion

Adrenalectomy is generally recommended for unilateral pheochromocytoma, overt adrenal Cushing syndrome, and lateralized primary aldosteronism. MACS requires individualized risk/benefit discussion.

Suspected ACC

Refer before biopsy or piecemeal operation to a high-volume multidisciplinary adrenal oncology center. Goal is intact en bloc R0 resection without tumor rupture; open surgery is generally favored for invasion or high oncologic concern.

Significant growth

For a nonoperated indeterminate lesion, ESE uses growth of >20% in maximum diameter plus ≥5 mm during 6–12 months as a threshold for surgical reconsideration. Smaller growth may justify another image depending on context. Measurement variability matters; compare identical planes/protocols when possible.

Biopsy: narrow indications

Consider only when all three are true: (1) the lesion is hormonally inactive and pheochromocytoma has been excluded; (2) imaging has not established benignity; and (3) histology will change management—classically a potentially solitary metastasis, lymphoma, or infection. Do not biopsy suspected ACC merely to “get tissue.”

Bilateral adrenal incidentalomas

Evaluate each lesion individually with the same imaging algorithm and perform the same hormonal assessment as for unilateral disease. Then identify the bilateral pattern:

  • Bilateral hyperplasia
  • Bilateral adenomas
  • Two non-adenoma-like masses
  • Different lesions on each side

Additional questions

  • Could this be bilateral macronodular adrenal hyperplasia, metastatic disease, lymphoma, infection, hemorrhage, congenital adrenal hyperplasia, or infiltrative disease?
  • Assess adrenal insufficiency when bilateral infiltrative/metastatic/hemorrhagic disease is present.
  • Measure 17-OHP when bilateral hyperplasia or large/bilateral myelolipomas suggests CAH.
  • In bilateral adenomas/hyperplasia with MACS, individualize management based on cortisol autonomy and comorbidity burden. Avoid bilateral adrenalectomy in the absence of overt Cushing syndrome because it creates permanent adrenal insufficiency.

Special situations

Known extra-adrenal malignancy

Pretest probability of metastasis is higher, so the classic incidentaloma assumptions do not apply. Still assess hormone excess—especially exclude pheochromocytoma before biopsy or resection. If the lesion is homogeneous and ≤10 HU, benignity is likely, though rare metastases can be low attenuation. FDG-PET/CT, interval imaging, or biopsy may be chosen based on whether the result changes cancer staging/therapy.

Age <40 years or pregnancy

Urgently assess because malignancy and clinically significant hormone excess are proportionally more likely. Prefer MRI when dedicated imaging is required during pregnancy. Surgical decisions in pregnancy require a specialized MDT and trimester-aware planning.

Frail patient or limited life expectancy

Scale testing to clinical gain. DST may be omitted when discovering MACS would not change management. Conversely, do not miss a pheochromocytoma if an invasive procedure is planned.

Myelolipoma

Typical imaging is usually diagnostic. Operate for symptoms/mass effect, hemorrhage, substantial growth, or diagnostic uncertainty rather than an inflexible size cutoff. Large and/or bilateral lesions warrant consideration of CAH. Surveillance practices vary because evidence is limited.

Adrenal cyst or hemorrhage

Confirm lack of enhancing solid components and consider interval imaging after hemorrhage resolves to exclude an underlying tumor. Complex cystic pheochromocytoma can be biochemically subtle. Bilateral hemorrhage is an adrenal-insufficiency emergency.

Genetic evaluation

Consider genetic counseling/testing for pheochromocytoma/paraganglioma (often broadly recommended), young age, bilateral/multifocal disease, family history, syndromic features, or ACC. Relevant syndromes include MEN2, VHL, NF1, SDHx-related disease, TMEM127/MAX, Li-Fraumeni, Lynch, FAP, Beckwith-Wiedemann, and Carney complex.

Calculators and quick tools

CT contrast washout

Enter all three values.

Traditional adenoma-compatible thresholds: APW >60% or RPW >40%. Do not use washout alone to exclude pheochromocytoma or malignancy.

Formulas

APW = 100 × (enhanced − delayed) ÷ (enhanced − precontrast)

RPW = 100 × (enhanced − delayed) ÷ enhanced

DST conversion

Cortisol µg/dL × 27.59 = nmol/L

1.8 µg/dL ≈ 50 nmol/L.

“Please characterize the adrenal lesion’s maximal diameter, homogeneity, noncontrast attenuation using an ROI excluding necrosis/hemorrhage, margins, macroscopic fat, calcification, enhancement, invasion/venous involvement, and comparison with all prior studies. Please clarify whether reported HU derives from a true unenhanced phase.”

Common errors that change care

  1. Using size as the entire algorithm. A 5-cm homogeneous 6-HU lesion and a 3-cm heterogeneous 35-HU lesion do not carry the same risk.
  2. Calling contrast-phase attenuation “native HU.” This invalidates lipid-rich classification.
  3. Repeating imaging forever. Clearly benign lesions do not need surveillance.
  4. Ordering annual hormone panels after a normal evaluation. Repeat only for new signs or worsening comorbidities.
  5. Calling every post-DST cortisol >1.8 µg/dL surgically actionable. First validate the test, prove ACTH independence, and judge comorbidity relevance.
  6. Using normal UFC or salivary cortisol to negate an abnormal DST in MACS. These tests answer different physiologic questions.
  7. Diagnosing unilateral primary aldosteronism from CT. Use AVS for most surgical candidates.
  8. Biopsying before excluding pheochromocytoma. This is the catastrophic error.
  9. Biopsying suspected ACC. It rarely answers the cortical adenoma-versus-carcinoma question and risks seeding/rupture.
  10. Ignoring bilateral adrenal insufficiency risk. Metastases, hemorrhage, infection, and infiltrative disease can destroy reserve.
  11. Sending suspected ACC to low-volume surgery. The first operation is the best chance for intact R0 resection.
