Primary Hyperparathyroidism

A clinically organized, high-yield guide that puts diagnosis, decision points, treatment, and monitoring first.

Focus: PHPTInteractive HTMLClinical study guide

Summary

Primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT) is hypercalcemia caused by excessive PTH secretion from one or more parathyroid glands. The PTH may be elevated or merely inappropriately normal for the degree of hypercalcemia. Most modern cases are mild and asymptomatic, but clinically silent kidney stones, vertebral fractures, osteoporosis, and hypercalciuria can still be present. Parathyroidectomy is the only curative treatment.
Guideline warning: Operative thresholds and monitoring recommendations evolve. Verify current society guidance before making clinical decisions.

Pathophysiology

Bone
  • PTH increases bone remodeling.
  • Chronic excess preferentially affects cortical bone.
  • Classic severe disease may produce subperiosteal resorption, osteitis fibrosa cystica, and brown tumors.
Kidney
  • Increases renal calcium reabsorption.
  • Promotes phosphate excretion.
  • Stimulates renal 1-alpha hydroxylase, increasing 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D.
Gut
  • Increased active vitamin D supports intestinal calcium absorption.
  • Net effect contributes to hypercalcemia.
Gland pathology
  • Single adenoma: most common.
  • Multigland hyperplasia or multiple adenomas: less common.
  • Parathyroid carcinoma: rare.

Diagnostic approach

Confirm hypercalcemia. Repeat total calcium with albumin correction or obtain ionized calcium when appropriate. Review dehydration, laboratory error, and medications.
Measure intact PTH. In hypercalcemia, a normal-range PTH is abnormal because physiologic PTH should be suppressed.
Classify as PTH-mediated or non-PTH-mediated. Nonsuppressed PTH supports PHPT, FHH, lithium-associated disease, thiazide-associated hypercalcemia, or tertiary HPT.
Exclude mimics and modifiers. Review lithium/thiazides, renal function, vitamin D status, malabsorption, calcium intake, and family history. Use urine calcium data to assess for FHH.
Assess target-organ involvement. Evaluate bone density/vertebral fractures and kidney stones/nephrocalcinosis before deciding on surgery or surveillance.
Localize only after the decision for surgery. Ultrasound, sestamibi/SPECT, 4D-CT, or other modalities are surgical road maps—not diagnostic tests.

PHPT versus familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia

Suggests PHPT
  • Newer-onset hypercalcemia
  • Urine calcium not low
  • Kidney stones or skeletal complications
  • No lifelong family pattern
Suggests FHH
  • Hypercalcemia from a young age
  • Family members with similar calcium pattern
  • Typically low urine calcium
  • CASR, GNA11, or AP2S1 pathway variant
Pitfall: Do not send a patient with unrecognized FHH to parathyroid surgery. The hypercalcemia usually persists because the disorder is systemic, not a single-gland surgical disease.

Normocalcemic primary hyperparathyroidism

Normocalcemic PHPT requires persistently normal total and ionized calcium with elevated PTH after secondary causes have been excluded. It is a diagnosis of exclusion, not a label for any patient with osteoporosis and a mildly high PTH.

Exclude before diagnosing
  • Vitamin D deficiency or insufficiency
  • Chronic kidney disease
  • Low calcium intake or malabsorption
  • Hypercalciuria
  • Medications that alter calcium/PTH physiology
  • Assay limitations and transient abnormalities
Diagnostic requirement: Correct vitamin D deficiency and confirm that the abnormality persists before diagnosing normocalcemic PHPT.

Clinical features

Most contemporary patients are asymptomatic. The absence of classic symptoms does not exclude clinically important skeletal or renal disease.

Renal

  • Nephrolithiasis
  • Nephrocalcinosis
  • Hypercalciuria
  • Polyuria/polydipsia in more marked hypercalcemia

Skeletal

  • Low BMD, especially cortical sites
  • Vertebral or nonvertebral fractures
  • Rare: osteitis fibrosa cystica, brown tumors

Neuropsychological

  • Fatigue, weakness, depression, cognitive complaints
  • These symptoms are nonspecific; evidence for predictable reversal after surgery is mixed.

Other associations

  • Hypertension and cardiovascular findings
  • Gout or pseudogout
  • Peptic ulcer disease or pancreatitis in severe disease
  • Many associations are epidemiologic rather than clearly causal.

Skeletal disease

Classic densitometric pattern: The distal one-third radius, a cortical site, is often most affected. Lumbar spine BMD may look relatively preserved because it is predominantly trabecular.
  • PTH excess increases both bone formation and resorption markers, reflecting high turnover.
  • Modern imaging shows that trabecular microarchitecture can still be impaired even when lumbar spine BMD is preserved.
  • Vertebral fracture risk can therefore be underestimated by lumbar spine BMD alone.
  • After successful surgery, bone resorption falls rapidly and BMD improves at multiple sites.

Renal disease

  • Stones may be symptomatic or clinically silent.
  • Renal imaging matters because history alone misses a meaningful number of patients.
  • Urinary calcium is not the only determinant of stone formation; interpret it with urine volume and other stone-risk factors.
  • Reduced kidney function can be both a consequence and a modifier of PHPT evaluation.

Parathyroidectomy

Operate on symptomatic PHPT. Examples include nephrolithiasis/nephrocalcinosis, fragility fracture or overt skeletal disease, or clinically significant hypercalcemia.

Surgical approaches

  • Bilateral four-gland exploration: traditional approach; high cure rate with an expert surgeon.
  • Minimally invasive parathyroidectomy: targeted operation when localization is convincing.
  • Intraoperative PTH: a drop of more than 50% at about 10 minutes, with an appropriate final value supports removal of the dominant hyperfunctioning source.

Immediate postoperative care

Expected
  • Rapid normalization of calcium and PTH
  • Fall in urinary calcium
  • Progressive BMD recovery
Watch for
  • Hypocalcemia
  • Hungry bone syndrome
  • Hypomagnesemia or hypophosphatemia
  • Recurrent laryngeal nerve injury and neck complications
Higher hungry-bone risk: severe skeletal disease, markedly elevated bone-turnover markers, very high PTH/alkaline phosphatase, and vitamin D deficiency.

Medical management when surgery is not performed

No single drug reproduces parathyroidectomy. Choose therapy based on the problem you are trying to solve.

InterventionMain effectWhat it does not reliably do
Hydration, mobility, medication reviewReduces avoidable worsening of hypercalcemiaDoes not cure PHPT
Normal guideline-level calcium intakeAvoids secondary PTH stimulation from unnecessary restrictionNot a treatment for the autonomous gland
Vitamin D repletionCan reduce PTH and support skeletal health when deficientRequires monitoring of calcium and urine calcium

Common error: Using cinacalcet to “treat osteoporosis.” It is primarily a calcium-lowering strategy. Use an antiresorptive when the target is BMD.

Special situations

Acute severe PHPT / hypercalcemic crisis
Severe hypercalcemia may present with dehydration, renal dysfunction, gastrointestinal symptoms, and altered mental status. Stabilize hypercalcemia aggressively, then pursue definitive surgery when feasible.
Pregnancy
PHPT in pregnancy carries maternal and fetal risk. Evaluation and timing of surgery require coordinated endocrine, surgical, and maternal-fetal medicine care.
Parathyroid carcinoma
Consider with very high calcium and PTH, severe renal/skeletal disease, a palpable neck mass, or an invasive/adherent gland. En bloc initial surgery offers the best chance for cure. Cinacalcet and antiresorptives can help control hypercalcemia when disease is unresectable.
Parathyromatosis
Scattered hyperfunctioning parathyroid tissue can arise after seeding from prior surgery or needle procedures. It often causes persistent or recurrent disease and is difficult to cure.
Inherited syndromes
Young age, multigland disease, recurrent disease, jaw tumors, pituitary/pancreatic tumors, medullary thyroid cancer, pheochromocytoma, or a strong family history should prompt consideration of a genetic syndrome.

Natural history

After surgery
  • Calcium and PTH normalize.
  • BMD generally improves at spine, hip, and radius.
  • Renal stone burden may fall.
Without surgery
  • Biochemistry and BMD may remain stable for years.
  • Some patients later develop rising calcium or cortical bone loss.
  • Long-term surveillance is therefore not optional.

Clinical pearls and pitfalls

  1. A normal PTH is not normal in hypercalcemia. It should be suppressed.
  2. Do not diagnose PHPT with imaging. Imaging finds the gland after the biochemical diagnosis and operative decision.
  3. Do not omit the distal one-third radius from DXA. PHPT preferentially affects cortical bone.
  4. Do not assume “asymptomatic” means no organ damage. Silent stones and vertebral fractures are common enough to look for.
  5. Normocalcemic PHPT is a diagnosis of exclusion. Vitamin D deficiency and CKD are far more common.
  6. Cinacalcet and bisphosphonates solve different problems. Cinacalcet lowers calcium; antiresorptives improve BMD.
  7. Think FHH before surgery. Especially in younger patients with lifelong mild hypercalcemia and low urine calcium.
  8. Guideline date matters. Operative thresholds and follow-up recommendations should be checked against the latest guidance.

Self-check

1. Why can a “normal” PTH support PHPT?
Because hypercalcemia should suppress PTH to a low level. A normal-range concentration is therefore inappropriately nonsuppressed.
2. Which DXA site is essential in PHPT but often omitted?
The distal one-third radius, because it is a cortical site and cortical bone is preferentially affected by chronic PTH excess.
3. What is the main role of parathyroid imaging?
Preoperative localization. It is not used to establish the biochemical diagnosis.
4. What laboratory pattern suggests secondary rather than primary HPT?
Elevated PTH with low or normal calcium, in the appropriate context such as CKD, vitamin D deficiency, low calcium intake, or malabsorption.
5. Which medication lowers calcium but usually does not improve BMD?
Cinacalcet.
6. Which therapy improves BMD but usually does not correct hypercalcemia?
An antiresorptive such as alendronate; denosumab is another option in selected patients.
7. What must be excluded before diagnosing normocalcemic PHPT?
Secondary causes of elevated PTH, especially vitamin D deficiency, CKD, low calcium intake/malabsorption, medication effects, and hypercalciuria.
8. What is the definitive treatment for PHPT?
Parathyroidectomy.
